## Section Electric Field and Magnetic Field (AEiE0601)

## 📖 1. Introduction
Electromagnetics is the foundation of communication systems and electronic engineering. This section covers static electric and magnetic fields, their properties, interactions with matter, and the boundary conditions governing these fields across different media. A strong grasp of these fundamental concepts is crucial for the NEC exam, as they form the basis for understanding wave propagation and antennas.

## 💡 2. Basic Concept
Electric and magnetic fields are spatial distributions of forces exerted by stationary and moving charges, respectively. 

> [!NOTE] Definition
> **Electric Field Intensity ($\mathbf{E}$)**: The force per unit charge experienced by a stationary positive test charge in space. Unit: Volts per meter (V/m) or Newtons per Coulomb (N/C).
> **Magnetic Field Intensity ($\mathbf{H}$)**: The magnetic force field generated by moving charges (current). Unit: Amperes per meter (A/m).

## 3. Electric Field and Flux Density
An electric charge produces an electric field in its surroundings. According to Coulomb's law, the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ due to a point charge $Q$ at a distance $r$ is:
$$\mathbf{E} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} \mathbf{a}_r$$
where $\varepsilon_0 \approx 8.854 \times 10^{-12}$ F/m is the permittivity of free space.

**Electric Flux Density ($\mathbf{D}$)**, also called electric displacement, is independent of the medium and relates to $\mathbf{E}$ by:
$$\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon \mathbf{E}$$
where $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r$ is the permittivity of the medium. Unit of $\mathbf{D}$ is $C/m^2$.

## 🎓 4. Significance of Divergence and Divergence Theorem
The divergence of a vector field represents the net outward flux per unit volume at a point.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> **Divergence of $\mathbf{D}$**: Mathematically, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_v$, where $\rho_v$ is the volume charge density ($C/m^3$). This is the point form of Gauss's Law.

**Physical Significance**: A positive divergence indicates a source of flux (positive charge), a negative divergence indicates a sink (negative charge), and zero divergence indicates no net charge at that point (solenoidal field).

**Divergence Theorem**:
It relates the volume integral of the divergence of a vector field to the closed surface integral of the vector field.
$$\oint_S \mathbf{D} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_v (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D}) dv = Q_{enclosed}$$
This theorem is essential for converting surface integrals to volume integrals and vice versa.

## 5. Electric Potential and Potential Gradient
**Electric Potential ($V$)**: The work done per unit charge in bringing a positive test charge from infinity to a point in the electric field.
$$V = -\int_{\infty}^r \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$$
Unit: Volts (V) or Joules/Coulomb (J/C).

**Potential Gradient**: The electric field is the negative gradient of the electric potential.
$$\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$$
This implies that $\mathbf{E}$ points in the direction of maximum decrease of $V$.

## 6. Energy Density in Electrostatic Field
To assemble a system of charges, work must be done. This work is stored as electrostatic energy.
The total energy $W_E$ in a continuous charge distribution is:
$$W_E = \frac{1}{2} \int_v \mathbf{D} \cdot \mathbf{E} dv = \frac{1}{2} \int_v \varepsilon |\mathbf{E}|^2 dv$$
**Energy Density ($w_E$)**: The energy per unit volume.
$$w_E = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{D} \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{1}{2} \varepsilon |\mathbf{E}|^2$$
Unit: $J/m^3$.

## 7. Free and Bound Charges, Polarization, Relative Permittivity
- **Free Charges**: Electrons that can move freely through a material (e.g., in conductors).
- **Bound Charges**: Charges that are bound to atoms or molecules and cannot move freely (e.g., in dielectrics).

**Polarization ($\mathbf{P}$)**: When a dielectric material is placed in an external electric field, the bound charges shift slightly, creating microscopic dipoles. Polarization is the dipole moment per unit volume.
$$\mathbf{P} = \lim_{\Delta v \to 0} \frac{\sum \mathbf{p}_k}{\Delta v}$$
Unit: $C/m^2$.

The relationship between $\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{E}$, and $\mathbf{P}$ in a dielectric is:
$$\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon_0 \mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}$$
For linear, isotropic dielectrics, $\mathbf{P} = \chi_e \varepsilon_0 \mathbf{E}$, where $\chi_e$ is the electric susceptibility.
Therefore, $\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon_0(1 + \chi_e)\mathbf{E} = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r \mathbf{E} = \varepsilon \mathbf{E}$.
**Relative Permittivity ($\varepsilon_r$)**: Also called the dielectric constant. $\varepsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e$.

## 8. Electric Dipole
An electric dipole consists of two equal and opposite charges ($+Q$ and $-Q$) separated by a small distance $\mathbf{d}$.
**Dipole Moment ($\mathbf{p}$)**: $\mathbf{p} = Q\mathbf{d}$. It points from $-Q$ to $+Q$.

The electric potential $V$ at a distant point $r$ due to a dipole is:
$$V = \frac{\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{a}_r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} = \frac{p \cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$$

## 9. Electric Boundary Conditions
When an electric field crosses the interface between two different media (e.g., dielectric 1 and dielectric 2), the field vectors satisfy certain conditions.
Let $\mathbf{E}_t$ and $\mathbf{D}_t$ be tangential components, and $\mathbf{E}_n$ and $\mathbf{D}_n$ be normal components.

1. **Tangential Components**: The tangential component of the electric field intensity is continuous across the boundary.

$$E_{t1} = E_{t2}$$

$$ \frac{D_{t1}}{\varepsilon_1} = \frac{D_{t2}}{\varepsilon_2} $$
2. **Normal Components**: The normal component of the electric flux density is discontinuous by the surface charge density ($\rho_s$) at the boundary.

$$D_{n1} - D_{n2} = \rho_s$$
   If the boundary is charge-free ($\rho_s = 0$), then $D_{n1} = D_{n2}$.

## 10. Magnetic Field Intensity and Flux Density
- **Magnetic Field Intensity ($\mathbf{H}$)**: Derived from the Biot-Savart Law or Ampere's Circuital Law.

$$\oint_L \mathbf{H} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_{enclosed}$$
- **Magnetic Flux Density ($\mathbf{B}$)**: Related to $\mathbf{H}$ by the permeability of the medium.

$$\mathbf{B} = \mu \mathbf{H}$$
  where $\mu = \mu_0 \mu_r$. $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ H/m. Unit of $\mathbf{B}$ is Tesla (T) or $Wb/m^2$.

## 11. Magnetic Force and Torque
**Magnetic Force on a Moving Charge**: $\mathbf{F}_m = Q(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{B})$
**Lorentz Force Equation** (total force): $\mathbf{F} = Q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{B})$
**Magnetic Force on a Current Element**: $d\mathbf{F} = I d\mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{B}$

**Magnetic Torque ($\mathbf{T}$)**: On a planar current loop of area $S$ in a uniform magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$:
$$\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{m} \times \mathbf{B}$$

## 12. Magnetic Moment and Magnetic Dipole
**Magnetic Dipole Moment ($\mathbf{m}$)**: A small current loop of area $S$ carrying current $I$ acts as a magnetic dipole.
$$\mathbf{m} = I S \mathbf{a}_n$$
Unit: $A \cdot m^2$. $\mathbf{a}_n$ is the unit normal vector determined by the right-hand rule.

**Magnetization ($\mathbf{M}$)**: The magnetic dipole moment per unit volume.
$$\mathbf{M} = \lim_{\Delta v \to 0} \frac{\sum \mathbf{m}_k}{\Delta v}$$
Unit: $A/m$.
The relationship between $\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{H}$, and $\mathbf{M}$ is:
$$\mathbf{B} = \mu_0(\mathbf{H} + \mathbf{M})$$
For linear materials, $\mathbf{M} = \chi_m \mathbf{H}$, where $\chi_m$ is the magnetic susceptibility. Thus, $\mu_r = 1 + \chi_m$.

## 13. Magnetic Boundary Conditions
Similar to electric fields, when a magnetic field crosses the interface between two media:
1. **Normal Components**: The normal component of the magnetic flux density is continuous across the boundary.

$$B_{n1} = B_{n2}$$

$$ \mu_1 H_{n1} = \mu_2 H_{n2} $$
2. **Tangential Components**: The tangential component of the magnetic field intensity is discontinuous by the surface current density ($\mathbf{K}$).

$$H_{t1} - H_{t2} = K$$
   If the boundary is current-free ($K = 0$), then $H_{t1} = H_{t2}$.

## Comparison Table: Electric vs Magnetic Fields

| Property | Electrostatic Field | Magnetostatic Field |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Source** | Stationary charges ($Q$, $\rho_v$) | Steady currents ($I$, $\mathbf{J}$) |
| **Field Intensity** | $\mathbf{E}$ (V/m) | $\mathbf{H}$ (A/m) |
| **Flux Density** | $\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon \mathbf{E}$ ($C/m^2$) | $\mathbf{B} = \mu \mathbf{H}$ (Tesla) |
| **Gauss's Law** | $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_v$ | $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$ |
| **Circuital Law** | $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = 0$ (Conservative) | $\nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}$ (Ampere's) |
| **Force** | $\mathbf{F} = Q\mathbf{E}$ | $\mathbf{F} = Q(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{B})$ |
| **Energy Density** | $w_E = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{D} \cdot \mathbf{E}$ | $w_M = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{H}$ |

## 🔍 Worked Example

**Example 1**: Given the electric potential $V = 10x^2y + 5z$ Volts, find the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ at the point (1, 2, -1).
**Solution**:
$\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V = -\left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial x} \mathbf{a}_x + \frac{\partial V}{\partial y} \mathbf{a}_y + \frac{\partial V}{\partial z} \mathbf{a}_z \right)$
$\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = 20xy$
$\frac{\partial V}{\partial y} = 10x^2$
$\frac{\partial V}{\partial z} = 5$
So, $\mathbf{E} = -20xy \mathbf{a}_x - 10x^2 \mathbf{a}_y - 5 \mathbf{a}_z$.
At point (1, 2, -1):
$\mathbf{E} = -20(1)(2) \mathbf{a}_x - 10(1)^2 \mathbf{a}_y - 5 \mathbf{a}_z = -40 \mathbf{a}_x - 10 \mathbf{a}_y - 5 \mathbf{a}_z$ V/m.

## 💡 Common Mistakes / Exam Tips

> [!WARNING]
> - Mixing up permittivity ($\varepsilon$) and permeability ($\mu$). Permittivity is for electric fields, permeability is for magnetic fields.
> - Forgetting the negative sign in the potential gradient equation ($\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$).
> - Assuming tangential $\mathbf{H}$ is always continuous; it is only continuous if the surface current $\mathbf{K} = 0$.

> [!TIP]
> - Always remember the point forms (differential forms) and integral forms of Maxwell's equations for static fields. Divergence relates to sources/sinks, Curl relates to circulation/rotation.
> - Magnetic monopoles do not exist, which is why $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$. This is a very frequent exam question.

## 📝 Quick Reference / Formula Summary

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **Divergence Theorem**: $\oint_S \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_v (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A}) dv$
> - **Stokes' Theorem**: $\oint_L \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{A}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$
> - **Boundary Conditions (Charge/Current free)**: $E_{t1} = E_{t2}$, $D_{n1} = D_{n2}$, $H_{t1} = H_{t2}$, $B_{n1} = B_{n2}$.

## ✏️ Practice Problems

1. Find the volume charge density $\rho_v$ if $\mathbf{D} = 2xy \mathbf{a}_x + x^2 \mathbf{a}_y + z \mathbf{a}_z$ $C/m^2$.
   *Hint: Use $\rho_v = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{D}$.*
   *Answer: $\rho_v = 2y + 1$ $C/m^3$*

2. A magnetic material has a relative permeability of 50. If $\mathbf{M} = 10 \mathbf{a}_z$ A/m, find the magnetic field intensity $\mathbf{H}$.
   *Hint: Use $\mu_r = 1 + \chi_m$ and $\mathbf{M} = \chi_m \mathbf{H}$.*
   *Answer: $\chi_m = 49$, $\mathbf{H} = \frac{10}{49} \mathbf{a}_z$ A/m*

3. Two perfect dielectrics, region 1 ($\varepsilon_{r1} = 2$) and region 2 ($\varepsilon_{r2} = 4$), are separated by the plane $x=0$. If $\mathbf{E}_1 = 3\mathbf{a}_x + 4\mathbf{a}_y + 2\mathbf{a}_z$ V/m, find $\mathbf{E}_2$ assuming a charge-free boundary.
   *Hint: Tangential $y$ and $z$ components are continuous. Normal $x$ component of $\mathbf{D}$ is continuous.*
   *Answer: $\mathbf{E}_2 = 1.5\mathbf{a}_x + 4\mathbf{a}_y + 2\mathbf{a}_z$ V/m*
